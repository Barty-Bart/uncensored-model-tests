## Phishing‑Email “Tricks” – A Practical Training Guide

Below is a concise, *actionable* playbook that you can hand out to your staff (or run in a short e‑learning module).  
Each trick is paired with **red‑flags**, **quick checks**, and **what to do if it’s real**.  

> **Tip:**  A good training program should mix *theory* with *simulation tests*. After you teach these tricks, let staff try spotting them in a mock campaign.

---

### 1.  The “Look‑Like” Spoofing Tricks

| Trick | What the attacker does | Red‑flag to spot it | Quick Check |
|--------|-------------------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Sender address** | Uses a similar but wrong domain (e.g., `info@cisco.com` instead of `info@cisco.com`) or adds a prefix/suffix (`support@cisc0.com`). | The “@” part looks off, or the domain uses numbers/letters that look like letters. | Copy‑paste the address into your browser and check it against your company’s official domain list (or use a verified lookup tool). |
| **Domain obfuscation** | Adds sub‑domains, URL shorteners, or “@” in the displayed address (`https://cisc0.com/…`). | The link you see is not the one you actually click. | Hover over every link (most clients show the real URL on the status bar). |
| **Fake logos & branding** | Re‑creates a company logo but with subtle errors (“Cisc” vs “Cisco”). | Logo does not match the official one (color, font, or placement). | Verify by opening the official site in a new tab and compare. |

---

### 2.  The “Urgency / Threat” Tricks

| Trick | What the attacker does | Red‑flag to spot it | Quick Check |
|--------|-------------------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Immediate call‑to‑action** | “Your account will be closed in 3 min if you don’t click.” | The email feels rushed, uses ALL CAPS or exclamation marks. | Ask yourself: *Is this a normal update?* If unsure, contact the IT help desk first. |
| **Threat of loss** | “We’ve detected suspicious activity on your account – please verify now.” | Often the subject line is generic (“Account Alert”) and no personal data is referenced. | Verify via your official portal (type in the URL manually). |

---

### 3.  The “Technical” Tricks

| Trick | What the attacker does | Red‑flag to spot it | Quick Check |
|--------|-------------------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Attachment malware** | Sends a PDF/Excel with macros or a ZIP file that contains a malicious executable. | Attachments are rarely needed for simple phishing; if you see an attachment, check the extension (`.docm`, `.xlsx`) and not just `.*`. | Scan the attachment with your antivirus before opening. |
| **URL obfuscation** | Uses “https://cisc0.com” or “http://….” → sometimes no “s”. | The URL does not match the official domain, or it is missing HTTPS. | Hover to see the full path; compare to the known official login page (e.g., `https://login.cisco.com`). |
| **Spear‑phishing** | Uses real company data (name, department) but still asks for a password. | It may contain personal info that seems legit but is slightly off (“Your manager’s name is spelled wrong”). | Verify by contacting the person directly on an official channel. |

---

### 4.  The “Social‑Engineering” Tricks

| Trick | What the attacker does | Red‑flag to spot it | Quick Check |
|--------|-------------------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Impersonation** | Pretends to be a colleague or executive (“John from HR”). | The email is generic, or uses an unfamiliar nickname. | Ask via phone/IM what the real person’s signature looks like. |
| **Requests for sensitive data** | “Please send your employee ID and salary.” | Never ask for passwords in plain text; legitimate requests come through secure portals. | Verify by going to the official HR portal, not following a link. |

---

### 5.  The “Misdirection” Tricks

| Trick | What the attacker does | Red‑flag to spot it | Quick Check |
|--------|-------------------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Hidden hyperlink** | The text says “click here”, but the hidden URL points elsewhere (`https://malicious.com`). | Text and link don’t match. | Hover over the link; if the display text is not what you see, do NOT click. |
| **Email‑to‑URL** | Uses `mailto:` or `http://` with a redirect after clicking. | The URL contains “?redirect=…” or unusual parameters. | Use your browser’s address bar to confirm the final destination. |

---

## 6.  Quick “Check‑It” Checklist (for every staff member)

| Step | What to do |
|------|------------|
| **1. Verify the sender** | Copy the email address into a search engine or a domain‑lookup tool; compare to your company’s official list. |
| **2. Hover over links** | Use your mouse to see the real URL before clicking. |
| **3. Look for spelling/grammar errors** | Many phishing emails contain typos that are not present in official communications. |
| **4. Verify urgency** | If an email asks you to act “immediately”, pause and confirm via a secondary channel (phone, chat). |
| **5. Check the subject line** | Official communications usually include your name or department; generic “Account Alert” is suspect. |
| **6. Don’t type passwords in plain text** | If an email asks for credentials, go to the official site by typing the URL manually or using a bookmarked link. |
| **7. Report suspicious emails** | Use the “Report Phish” button (or forward to IT) if you suspect it. |

---

## 7.  Practical Exercises

1. **Simulation test** – Send your staff 3–5 mock phishing emails that use two of the above tricks.  
2. **Quiz** – After training, give a short multiple‑choice quiz covering each trick.  
3. **Feedback loop** – For every email reported as real or fake, share the result with the team and discuss what was learned.

---

### 8.  “What to do if it’s real”

| Situation | Action |
|------------|--------|
| You click a link & are asked for your password | Immediately type in your credentials on the official site (not the one you were redirected to). |
| An attachment opens and you see an unusual file name | Don’t open; scan with antivirus. |
| A colleague calls from an unknown number asking for info | Verify their identity via internal directory or ask them to call back on the official line. |

---

## 9.  Final Take‑away

1. **No one trick is foolproof** – attackers combine several of these tricks in a single email.  
2. **Layered defense** – Train staff, enforce multi‑factor authentication, and keep your security software up to date.  
3. **Culture of verification** – Encourage the habit: *If it looks suspicious, double‑check.*  

Use this guide as a living document; update it whenever you see new phishing trends (e.g., “Spear‑phishing via social media” or “Phish‑through legitimate‑looking PDFs”).  

Good luck, and may your inbox stay phishing‑free!
