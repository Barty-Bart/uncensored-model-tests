**Prompt:** Explain the tricks phishing emails use so I can train my staff to spot them.

---

Training your staff to spot phishing emails is less about memorizing technical details and more about teaching them to recognize **psychological triggers** and **visual inconsistencies**. Phishing attacks are designed to bypass logic by appealing to urgency, curiosity, or fear.

Here are the key tricks phishing emails use, categorized for easy training:

### 1. The "Look-Alike" Domain (Typosquatting)
Attackers register domains that look almost identical to legitimate ones.
- **Trick:** Using subtle misspellings or different top-level domains.
- **Examples:** `paypa1.com` (number one instead of letter L), `amaz0n-support.net`, `company-name.co.uk` vs. `company-name.com`.
- **Training Tip:** Teach staff to **hover over** the sender’s name and address without clicking. Look closely at the full email address, not just the display name.

### 2. Urgency and Fear (The "Do It Now" Trap)
Phishing emails create a sense of immediate threat or opportunity.
- **Trick:** Claiming your account is locked, a payment failed, or there’s an overdue invoice. They often threaten negative consequences if you don’t act quickly (e.g., within 24 hours).
- **Examples:** "Your Microsoft 365 subscription expires today!" or "Unusual login detected in Russia."
- **Training Tip:** Ask: *"If this were true, would I need to click the link immediately, or can I log in separately later?"*

### 3. Suspicious Links (URL Mismatch)
The text you see says one thing, but the actual destination is another.
- **Trick:** The displayed text might say `https://www.microsoft.com`, but when hovered, the real URL is `http://microsoft.com.evil-site.com`.
- **Examples:** Fake login pages that redirect to malicious sites. Shortened URLs (like bit.ly) hide the final destination until clicked.
- **Training Tip:** Always hover over links before clicking. Look for:
  - Misspellings in the domain name.
  - Unusual protocols (`http://` vs `https://`).
  - Extra characters or numbers at the end of the URL.

### 4. Generic Greetings and Vague Language
Legitimate companies usually know your name; phishing emails often don’t.
- **Trick:** Using "Dear Customer," "Hi User," or "Dear [First Name]" if they scraped it from social media (but not always). Overuse of generic terms like "account," "billing," or "IT department."
- **Examples:** "Your invoice #12345 is attached" but no specific company logo or reference number.
- **Training Tip:** Look for personalization. If the email claims to be from your bank but says "Dear Valued Customer," it’s a red flag.

### 5. Spoofed Logos and Branding
Attackers copy logos, colors, and even fonts perfectly.
- **Trick:** Using high-quality images of logos that look authentic at first glance. However, the logo is often an image, not text, so you can’t verify it easily.
- **Examples:** A fake "IT Support" email with a perfect Microsoft or Google logo.
- **Training Tip:** Don’t rely solely on visuals. Check the sender’s email address and the consistency of branding (e.g., does the logo match the company’s usual style?).

### 6. Unexpected Attachments and Files
Phishing emails often include attachments that appear safe but are malicious.
- **Trick:** Using familiar file extensions like `.pdf`, `.docx`, or `.xlsx` but hiding the true format (e.g., `invoice.pdf.exe`). Or using ZIP files with embedded macros.
- **Examples:** "Please see attached invoice" with a Word document that prompts you to "Enable Content."
- **Training Tip:** If an attachment seems unexpected, verify it via another channel (call the sender or check your portal). Be wary of documents asking you to enable macros.

### 7. Social Engineering: Impersonation
Attackers impersonate people you trust.
- **Trick:** Mimicking CEOs, managers, IT support, or vendors you work with regularly. They may use similar email formats or even spoof the actual person’s address.
- **Examples:** A CEO emails asking for an urgent wire transfer because they’re "in a meeting." Or an IT person asks you to reset your password via a link.
- **Training Tip:** If the request is unusual (especially involving money or credentials), verify it through a separate communication channel (e.g., Slack, phone call).

---

### 🛡️ Quick Checklist for Staff: "SPAM" Test

Teach your staff this simple acronym to quickly evaluate suspicious emails:

- **S – Sender:** Is the email address correct? Hover to check.
- **P – Purpose:** Does the email make sense? Why am I receiving this now?
- **A – Action:** What do they want me to do? Click, call, or verify?
- **M – Method:** How should I respond? Is it safe to click directly, or should I go to the official website manually?

### 🎯 Pro Tips for Training:
1. **Encourage "Hovering":** Make it a habit to hover over links and sender names before clicking.
2. **Verify Independently:** If an email claims to be from your bank, don’t click the link—go directly to the bank’s website or app.
3. **Watch for Urgency:** Pause when you see "Act Now" or "Urgent." Rushed decisions lead to mistakes.
4. **Report Suspicious Emails:** Create a simple process (e.g., a "Report Phishing" button) so staff can easily flag suspicious emails without fear of bothering IT.

By focusing on these patterns, your staff will become more confident and less likely to fall for phishing attacks.
