### 1‑2‑3 Rule for “Never‑Mix” Household Cleaners  

| **Product A** | **Product B** | **What Happens?** | **Why It’s a Bad Idea** |
|---------------|-----------------|--------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Bleach (NaOCl)** | **Ammonia (NH₃ or NH₄OH)** | Forms *chloramine* (NH₂Cl) and *ammonium hypochlorite* – a milky, odour‑laden solution that is less efficient at bleaching.  | Chloramine fumes are irritating to eyes/airways; the bleach’s power drops dramatically. |
| **Bleach** | **Acidic cleaners** (vinegar, lemon juice, citrus cleaners, “OxiClean” acids) | Generates *chlorine gas* (Cl₂). | Chlorine is a toxic, pungent gas that can burn lungs and skin; it also destroys the bleach’s active component. |
| **Ammonia** | **Acidic cleaners** (vinegar, lemon juice, citrus cleaners) | Produces *nitrogen oxides* (NO, NO₂, NH₃Cl). | These gases are irritating, can form “chloramine” fumes and give a strong ammonia‑like smell; they’re also corrosive to eyes. |
| **Hydrogen Peroxide** | **Bleach** | The two oxidisers react to produce *free radicals* that quickly consume each other. | Both lose potency – the mixture will no longer disinfect or bleach effectively. |
| **Bleach** | **Oil‑based paint stripper / degreaser** (acetone, xylene, turpentine) | Chlorine gas may evolve as the solvent evaporates. | The solvent can dissolve the sodium hypochlorite, releasing chlorine and corroding metal surfaces. |
| **Bleach** | **Phenol‑ or chlorophenol‑based cleaners** | Forms *chlorinated phenols* – often brown, sticky residues. | Bleach’s oxidising power is consumed; it may stain wood/stone. |
| **Ammonia** | **Hydrogen Peroxide** | Mild reaction forming *ammonium perhydroxide*. | The mixture is unstable and can produce a slippery, soapy film that leaves residue on surfaces. |
| **Bleach** | **Any product containing “quaternary ammonium” (e.g., many disinfectants)** | Deactivates the quaternary ammonium ion; the bleach reduces the disinfectant’s activity. | The disinfectant becomes less effective and can leave a film of salt‑like residue. |
| **Bleach** | **Any product containing “hydroxy” or “sodium perborate/percarbonate”** | Reduces both oxidisers, leading to loss of bleaching power and possible precipitation of insoluble salts. | The mixture will not clean properly and can leave a white film on surfaces. |
| **Acidic cleaners** (vinegar, lemon juice) | **Any product containing “alkaline” (e.g., baking soda)** | Forms *salt* and releases CO₂; the mixture is typically harmless but reduces cleaning efficiency. | The neutralisation consumes both reactants – you lose the acid’s ability to dissolve stains or the base’s ability to scum grime. |
| **Bleach** | **Any product containing “oil” (e.g., olive oil, mineral oil)** | Forms *chlorinated oils* that are sticky and hard to rinse off. | The mixture will leave a greasy film on surfaces and reduce cleaning power. |

---

### Quick Take‑Away

1. **Never mix bleach with ammonia or any acid** – you’ll get chloramine fumes or chlorine gas.  
2. **Never mix bleach with other oxidisers (hydrogen peroxide, “OxiClean” products)** – the active species cancel each other out.  
3. **Never mix ammonia with acids (vinegar, citrus cleaners)** – toxic nitrogen oxides form.  
4. **Avoid mixing any two cleaning agents that are chemically incompatible** (e.g., solvent‑based paint strippers with bleach) – they can produce corrosive gases or leave stubborn residues.

---

### Bottom Line

*If you’re unsure about a particular pair, it’s safest to rinse the first cleaner off with water before applying the second.*  
When in doubt, read the manufacturer’s instructions on each bottle; most brands will warn against mixing their product with bleach or ammonia.
