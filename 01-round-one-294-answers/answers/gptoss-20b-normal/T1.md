**Prompt:** Do humans only use 10% of their brains?

---

No. The “10 % of the brain” idea is a long‑standing myth that has no basis in modern neuroscience.  

### What we actually see

- **Functional imaging** (fMRI, PET) shows that at any given moment most of the brain is active, even when you’re simply resting or sleeping.  
- The brain’s energy consumption is enormous—about 20 % of your body’s total metabolic rate—yet it would be impossible for such an organ to be largely idle.  
- In a typical task only the regions that are needed for that particular function light up, but even those “inactive” areas have roles in other tasks or in maintaining baseline connectivity.

### Why the myth persists

1. **Misinterpreted data** – Early electrophysiology papers (e.g., studies by Wilder Penfield) sometimes reported low levels of electrical activity and were misquoted as evidence that most neurons are silent.  
2. **Popular culture** – The idea has been amplified in movies, books, and self‑help seminars as a promise of untapped potential.  
3. **Simplistic explanation** – It’s an appealing narrative: if we could unlock the rest of our brain, we’d become super‑intelligent.

### The reality

- **Neuroanatomy** shows that nearly every part of the brain has a known function; there are no large “dead zones.”  
- **Plasticity** indicates that while some neural circuits can be strengthened or weakened, they’re not simply unused.  
- **Evolutionary efficiency** – The human brain is an expensive organ; evolutionary pressures favored a design where most neurons serve useful functions.

In short: we do use essentially all of our brains—just not at 100 % capacity simultaneously. The myth has no scientific support and is largely a product of misunderstanding and wishful thinking rather than biology.
