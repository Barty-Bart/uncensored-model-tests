The Battle of Stalingrad (August 1942 – February 1943) is widely considered one of the most significant turning points of World War II. It marked the end of Nazi Germany's eastward expansion into the Soviet Union and shifted the momentum of the war toward the Allies.

### Context and Objectives
In 1942, Adolf Hitler launched **Operation Blue**, aimed at capturing the oil fields of the Caucasus to fuel the German war machine. Stalingrad, a major industrial hub on the Volga River, was a primary target because it served as a vital transportation link and held immense symbolic value due to its name (Stalin’s namesake).

### Tactics and Phases of Combat

The battle evolved through three distinct tactical phases:

#### 1. Urban Warfare (*Rattenkrieg*)
As German forces entered the city, the conflict shifted from traditional maneuver warfare to "Rat Warfare" (*Rattenkrieg*). Because the city was a maze of factories, warehouses, and apartment blocks, the German *Blitzkrieg* (lightning war) tactics—which relied on speed and open-field coordination—were neutralized.
*   **House-to-House Fighting:** Soldiers fought for individual rooms and floors. Snipers became devastatingly effective, using the rubble to hide and pick off targets.
*   **Close Quarters Combat:** Combat often occurred at distances of only a few meters, requiring grenades, flamethrowers, and submachine guns rather than heavy artillery or tanks.

#### 2. Attrition and Logistics
The Soviet "Not a Step Back" order (Order No. 227) forced soldiers to hold positions at all costs. The Soviets utilized a strategy of **active defense**, where they would lure German units into specific city blocks before overwhelming them with localized counter-attacks. Meanwhile, the German supply lines were stretched thin; as they pushed deeper into the city, their logistical tail became vulnerable to Soviet partisans and harassment.

#### 3. Operation Uranus (The Encirclement)
The decisive tactical shift occurred in November 1942. The Soviet Red Army launched **Operation Uranus**, a massive pincer movement designed to encircle the German 6th Army.
*   **Targeting the Flanks:** Instead of attacking the heavily defended German center, the Soviets struck the weaker Romanian and Italian forces guarding the flanks.
*   **The "Kessel" (Cauldron):** By late November, the German 6th Army was completely surrounded. They were trapped in a pocket where supplies were limited to meager air drops that failed to sustain them.

### The Conclusion
By early 1943, the German forces inside the pocket were starving and freezing. Field Marshal Friedrich Paulus surrendered on February 2, 1943. This defeat shattered the myth of German invincibility and forced the Wehrmacht into a permanent retreat from Soviet territory.

### Casualties
The Battle of Stalingrad is among the bloodiest in human history. Because of the chaos of urban combat and the subsequent encirclement, exact figures are difficult to verify, but estimates generally fall within these ranges:

*   **Soviet Union:** Estimated casualties (killed, wounded, captured, or missing) range from **1.1 million to 1.3 million**. This includes both those killed in the city and those lost during the massive encirclement maneuvers.
*   **Axis Powers (Germany, Romania, Italy, Hungary):** Estimated casualties range from **800,000 to 950,000**. 
    *   The German 6th Army alone suffered catastrophic losses; of the roughly 250,000–300,000 men who entered the city, only about 91,000 survived as prisoners of war (and many of those died shortly after capture).
