The history of the bicycle dates back to the early 19th century, with the first prototype emerging in 1817. German inventor Karl von Drais created a wooden contraption with no pedals, known as the "draisine" or "dandy horse." Riders would propel it by pushing with their feet along the ground. This early model was the precursor to the modern bicycle.

Over the next several decades, innovations and improvements were made to the design. In 1830, Scottish inventor Kirkpatrick MacMillan added pedals to the front wheel, creating a more efficient mode of transportation. The first two-wheeled, pedal-powered bicycle was born. However, it wasn't until the mid-1800s that the bicycle gained popularity, particularly among the upper class in Europe.

The introduction of the "penny-farthing" or "high wheeler" in the 1870s revolutionized the design. This model featured a large front wheel and a smaller rear wheel, allowing for greater speed and efficiency. The penny-farthing became a symbol of freedom and adventure, with cycling clubs and competitions emerging across Europe and North America.

The late 1800s saw significant advancements in bicycle technology, including the introduction of pneumatic tires, chain-driven gears, and the "safety bicycle" design. This design, developed by John Kemp Starley in 1885, featured equal-sized wheels and a chain-driven mechanism, making it more accessible and user-friendly for the masses.

The early 20th century saw the rise of mass production and the development of new materials, such as aluminum and steel. The bicycle became an affordable and popular mode of transportation, with millions of units being produced worldwide. Today, bicycles come in a wide range of styles and designs, from road bikes to mountain bikes, and continue to be a beloved mode of transportation and recreation for people around the world.
